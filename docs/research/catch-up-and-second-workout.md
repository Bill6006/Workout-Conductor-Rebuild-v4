# Items 12 and 32: a catch-up on an unplanned day, and a second workout on the same day

_Research report, 2026-10-10, for Round M (Maintenance 31). Carried into the repository on 2026-10-17 for the cloud handoff (docs/handoff/CLOUD_HANDOFF.md). EB = evidence-based, PJ = product judgement._

The raw notes and saved abstracts stayed on the working machine and are not in the repository; every claim below cites its PubMed ID (or DOI) so it can be checked again.

**Method.** Every PubMed claim below was checked against its abstract, fetched with curl and E-utilities (unsandboxed). I also read the full text of the ACSM 2026 position stand from PMC (PMC12965823). One source is a preprint that is not in PubMed, and it is labelled where it appears. No project files were touched.

### Bottom line
- **What drives gains is weekly sets per muscle.** Spreading those sets over more days does not matter for size and helps strength a little. So a catch-up session is worth exactly the sets it adds. Because returns diminish, sets for the muscles furthest behind are worth the most.
- **No trial has added a second full dose to a muscle already trained that day.** Splitting one day's dose across two sessions works fine in trained lifters. Sets taken at or near failure leave a muscle below baseline for 24 to 48 h, and up to 72 h for squats. So "go easy" should normally mean the muscle sits out.
- **Missing a session costs little.** Catching up is reasonable but optional. No study compares catching up with simply carrying on.
- **Novices are the group to protect.** They take more muscle damage, recover more slowly, and no twice-daily loaded-training study has included them.

### 1. Training frequency vs weekly volume
- **Schoenfeld, Ogborn & Krieger 2016 (PMID 27102172; 10 studies).** With volume equated, training a muscle twice a week beat once a week for hypertrophy. The 2019 analysis superseded this.
- **Schoenfeld, Grgic & Krieger 2019 (30558493; 25 studies).** With volume equated, frequency made no significant difference. That held for direct measures, trained subjects, and upper and lower body. Studies that did not equate volume favoured higher frequency, but only modestly.
- **Grgic et al. 2018 (29470825; 22 studies).** Strength effect sizes rose with frequency: 0.74, 0.82, 0.93 and 1.08 for 1, 2, 3 and 4+ sessions a week. In the volume-equated subgroup there was no frequency effect (p = 0.421). The authors conclude the effect is "primarily driven by training volume".
- **Pelland et al. 2026 (41343037; 67 studies, 2,058 participants).**
  - More volume increases both hypertrophy and strength (posterior probability 100%), with diminishing returns that are stronger for strength.
  - Frequency's effect on hypertrophy is "compatible with negligible effects".
  - Frequency's effect on strength is positive (100%), with diminishing returns.
  - Counting indirect sets as 0.5 fit the data best.
- **Currier et al. 2026, ACSM position stand (41843416; 137 systematic reviews, more than 30,000 participants; full text read).**
  - Hypertrophy improves with at least 10 sets per muscle per week. It was not affected by 1 versus more than 5 days a week when volume was equated.
  - Strength improves with at least 2 sessions a week, 2 to 3 sets, loads of at least 80% 1RM, and the exercise placed at the start of the session.
  - Frequency's effect on strength "is diminished when volume is equated".
  - Training to failure did not consistently change outcomes.
- **Other trials agree:**
  - Ramos-Campo 2024 (38595233; 14 studies, 392 people): split and full-body routines gave equal results.
  - Saric 2019 (30363041; n=27, trained) and Colquhoun 2018 (29324578; n=28, trained): 3 and 6 sessions a week gave equal results.
  - Zaroni 2019 (31260419; n=18): training each muscle 5 times a week gave more growth in some muscles.
  - Cuthbert 2021 (33886099): no clear frequency effect on strength in well-trained people.
- **Dose-response:**
  - Schoenfeld 2017 (27433992; 15 studies): each extra weekly set was associated with about 0.37% more growth.
  - Hammarström 2020 (31813190; n=34 untrained, one leg per condition): 3 sets per exercise grew muscle area 5.2% against 3.7% for 1 set.
- **Implication for Item 12:** the gain comes from the added sets, not from the extra day. If the catch-up only moves sets earlier in the week without adding any, the evidence says that is neutral.

### 2. The same muscle twice in one day, or within 24 h
**Two-a-day trials.** All were in trained athletes, all were small, and all split a fixed daily volume rather than adding a second dose.
- **Häkkinen & Kallinen 1994 (8187678; n=10 female athletes).** A 3-week once-daily period produced no change. A second 3-week period with the same volume split into two daily sessions raised strength 5% (2,493 to 2,620 N) and increased quadriceps cross-sectional area. The periods ran in sequence, and the abstract does not say they were counterbalanced.
- **Häkkinen & Pakarinen 1991 (1761007; n=9 male strength athletes).** Same design and pattern: maximal force rose 5% only in the two-a-day period.
- **Häkkinen et al. 1988 (3253232; n=8 elite weightlifters, one week of two sessions a day).** Strength dropped after each session, but resting strength did not change over the week.
- **Hartman et al. 2007 (19124903; n=10 national-level weightlifters, 3 weeks).** No significant differences between once and twice daily.
- **Corrêa et al. 2022 (33634677; n=23 trained men, 8 weeks).** Training each muscle twice daily raised squat 1RM 16.1% against 7.8%. Hypertrophy, bench press and muscular endurance were similar.

**How long recovery takes:**
- **Raastad & Hallén 2000 (10929214; n=10 strength athletes).**
  - A heavy 90-minute leg session (3RM and 6RM loads) needed 33 h to recover, with a second dip in performance at 11 to 22 h.
  - The same session at 70% of those loads had recovered by 3 h.
- **Raastad et al. 2003 (12832432; n=10).** Recovery stalled between 10 and 23 h after the workout, at the same time as white blood cells built up in the muscle.
- **González-Badillo 2016 (26667923; n=9).** Doing 3 sets of 4 reps out of 8 possible: jump height recovered by 6 h. Doing 3 sets of 8 to failure: jump height was still down at 48 h.
- **Failure slows recovery:**
  - Morán-Navarro 2017 (28965198; n=10 trained): up to 24 to 48 h.
  - Pareja-Blanco 2020 (30036284; n=10): function stayed depressed up to 48 h, especially with high-rep sets.
  - Vieira 2022 meta-analysis (34881412): failure caused more acute fatigue and higher CK at 48 h.
- **Refalo 2023 (36752989; n=24 trained, 6 sets of bench press).** At 24 h, bar speed was 3% lower after sets to failure or to 1 RIR, but 2% higher after sets stopped at 3 RIR. All differences were gone by 48 h.
- **Goulart 2021 (32594858; n=14 trained; 5 sets of 8 to 10RM to failure on squat and leg press).** Volume load was still down at 24 h; it took at least 48 h to restore.
- **Belcher 2019 (30779596; n=12 well-trained; 4 sets to failure at 80%).** Squat bar speed stayed down for up to 72 h. Bench press was down only immediately after, and deadlift not at all.
- **Muscle protein synthesis.** MacDougall 1995 (8563679; n=6) found it up 109% at 24 h and back near baseline by 36 h. Phillips 1997 (9252485; n=8 untrained) found it still up 34% at 48 h.
- **Daily training at moderate volume per session is fine.** See Saric and Colquhoun above. In Raastad 2003 (12753488), two weeks of daily leg training reduced the fatigue a test workout caused.

**Performance later the same day:**
- **Low-volume heavy work helps or is neutral:**
  - Cook 2014 (23707139; n=18): a morning of 3RM bench press and squat improved strength, sprint and jump in the afternoon.
  - Mason 2020 systematic review (32779102; 29 studies): heavy low-volume resistance work improved strength and power 4 to 6 h later, less so at 1 to 3 h or 6 to 12 h.
  - Harrison 2021 (33761461; n=15): squat jump was 4.5% higher 8 h after heavy half-squats.
  - Riemann 2024 (38736603; n=15): 2 sets of 10 at 70 to 80% on three leg exercises left afternoon jump height unchanged 4 to 6 h later.
- **Two hard sessions hurt:** Goto 2017 (28674872; n=11) found two strenuous sessions 4 h apart reduced jump performance.
- **Other muscles are barely affected.** Behm 2021 (33818751; 52 studies, 303 people): fatiguing one muscle has a trivial effect on other muscles' strength and power (effect size −0.02 overall). The effect on endurance is moderate (−0.54).

**Repeated bout effect:**
- **A first bout protects against damage from later ones:**
  - Hyldahl 2017 (27782911) reviews the mechanisms.
  - Doma 2023 (38015738; 20 studies) shows this for common multi-joint exercises.
- **A second bout 2 to 6 days into recovery does not worsen damage, though it reduces the work done:**
  - Nosaka & Newton 2002 (11834116).
  - Chen 2003 (12665973; n=26).
  - Chen & Hsieh 2000 (10999263; n=24).
- **A second bout at 24 h did add damage.** Morin 2023 (37351697; n=30): repeating maximal eccentric knee-extensor work a day later left strength 14.5% down at 48 h, against 6.6% without the repeat.

**Animal evidence.** In rats, Takegaki 2019 (31250976) found that maximal bouts every 72 h or every 24 h produced hypertrophy. Bouts every 8 h produced none, with protein breakdown and inflammation. The stimulus was extreme and electrically evoked. In humans, a low-load blood-flow-restriction block of 23 sessions in 19 days still grew muscle (Nielsen 2012, 22802591), so the rat result should not be over-read.

**Guidelines.** No position stand gives a rule for training the same muscle twice in one day: not ACSM 2009 (19204579), 2011 (21694556) or 2026.

**Answer to question 2.**
- Splitting one day's normal dose across two sessions is supported, in trained lifters.
- Adding hard sets to a muscle already taken near failure that day is not supported:
  - recovery takes 24 to 48 h or more;
  - repeating damaging work within 24 h adds damage;
  - returns from extra sets diminish.
- No study says how much to cut back.

### 3. Short sessions
- **ACSM 2026.** It advises at least two sets per exercise. More sets may add benefit, but each adds less. "Minimal doses" bring substantial gains.
- **Androulakis-Korakakis 2020 (31797219; 6 studies, trained men).** One set to failure, 2 to 3 times a week, produced significant (though suboptimal) 1RM gains.
- **Iversen 2021 (34125411).** Recommends at least 4 sets per muscle per week, and says weekly volume matters more than frequency.
- **Fyfe 2022 (34822137).** Minimal-dose training works and may limit the negative feelings that higher volumes bring.
- **Maintenance:**
  - Bickel 2011 (21131862; n=70): cutting the dose to 1/3 or 1/9 kept hypertrophy for 32 weeks in young adults, but not in older adults.
  - Spiering 2021 (33629972): 1 session a week with 1 set per exercise maintains strength and size for up to 32 weeks if the load is kept. Older people need 2 sessions and 2 to 3 sets.
- **Preprint, not peer reviewed, no PMID: Remmert et al. 2025, SportRxiv.**
  - Per-session returns flatten at about 2 direct sets for strength and about 11 fractional sets for hypertrophy.
  - The hypertrophy model covered 35 studies and 1,032 people; the average session had about 6 sets.
  - Data at high volumes per session are sparse.
- **Answer:** a short session of 2 to 3 sets per lagging muscle is supported. No study tests "a short session built from lagging muscles" as such.

### 4. Catching up a missed session vs continuing the plan
- **No study compares the two directly.**
- **Breaks cost little:**
  - Coleman 2024 (38274324; n=39 trained): a 1-week break mid-programme left hypertrophy unchanged; strength gains were smaller.
  - Hwang 2017 (28328712; n=20 trained): strength was kept over a 2-week break.
  - Ogasawara 2013 (23053130; n=14): 3-week breaks every 6 weeks gave the same results at 24 weeks.
  - Halonen 2024 (39364857; n=42 untrained): what was lost in a 10-week break came back quickly.
  - Bosquet 2013 (23347054; 103 studies): losses grow with the length of the break, and are larger in people over 65 and in inactive people.
- **Choosing the day flexibly works:**
  - McNamara 2010 (19858756; n=16 beginners): choosing on the day which workout to do gave a larger leg-press gain.
  - Colquhoun 2017 (28129275; n=25 trained): a flexible session order gave equal results.
- **Inference:** catching up restores weekly volume, which is what matters, and when it is done does not. It is reasonable if recovery allows, and skipping costs little. So the app should offer a catch-up, not push one.

### 5. Is a second session the same day safe for novices?
- **No study of twice-daily loaded training in novices was found.**
- **Untrained muscle is damaged more and recovers more slowly:**
  - Newton 2008 (18550979; n=30): untrained men were still about 40% weaker on day 3, while trained men had recovered.
  - Pareja-Blanco 2019 (30722654; n=10): the same relative session caused slower recovery and more damage before 10 weeks of training than after.
  - Damas 2016 (27219125; n=10): damage was highest in week 1, lower by week 3, and minimal by week 10.
- **Exertional rhabdomyolysis.** Unaccustomed exercise is a precipitating cause (Rawson 2017, 28332112). Weightlifting accounted for 14.8% of 772 cases in one systematic review (Bäcker 2023, 36877581). It is rare but serious.
- **Resistance training is safe overall.** The ACSM 2026 full text cites an analysis of more than 38,000 participants with no increase in serious adverse events.
- **Brief bodyweight sessions several times a day were safe in untrained older adults.** Fyfe 2022 (35751032): 9-minute "exercise snacks" 1, 2 or 3 times a day produced 2 adverse events in 1,317 sessions. Adherence fell from 97% to 82% to 81% as sessions per day rose. In Perkin 2019 (31687210), twice-daily snacks improved leg function.
- **Answer:** the concern is a novice training the same muscles again hours later. A second session for other muscles at moderate effort raises no recognised concern.

### What the evidence can and cannot decide
**It can decide:**
- Weekly volume is the lever. Spreading it over more days is neutral for size and slightly positive for strength.
- The first sets for an under-dosed muscle are worth the most.
- At least 2 sets per session is the supported floor.
- Work at or near failure (0 to 1 RIR) needs 24 to 48 h, or up to 72 h for squats. Work stopped around 3 RIR, or at about half the possible reps, recovers within hours to a day.
- Splitting one day's dose in two is fine for trained lifters.
- The first session does not meaningfully weaken muscles it did not train.
- One missed session costs little.

**It cannot decide** (these go to the owner):
- 2 vs 3 vs 4 sets per muscle in a catch-up.
- Whether extra afternoon sets help or harm a muscle fully trained that morning. There is no human trial.
- The exact RIR or volume cut for a muscle trained twice in one day.
- Whether catching up beats skipping.
- Where the novice cut-off lies.

### Recommended defaults
**Item 12: catch-up on an unplanned day**
1. Show the one line and offer the catch-up, with the full session one tap away. Never present the catch-up as needed.
2. **Which muscles:** only muscles that are behind and outside the 48-h sit-out. Keep the 48-h rule here: near-failure work depresses performance for 24 to 48 h, and a damaging repeat at 24 h adds damage.
3. **Order:** largest relative shortfall first, with each muscle's main lift first. Strength benefits from early placement; size is unaffected.
4. **Sets:**
   - 3 working sets per behind muscle for non-novices, 2 for novices, never fewer than 2.
   - Never more than the muscle's remaining weekly deficit, or than its normal sets in one planned session.
5. **Effort:** the app's normal RIR, with no sets to failure. Novices stay at 2 to 3 RIR.

**Item 32: second workout on the same day**
1. **Lead with** behind muscles that had no direct sets today and are outside the 48-h window. Dose them as in Item 12.
2. **Muscles already trained today:**
   - Cap the day's total for each muscle at one normal session's dose. Count today's sets, with indirect sets as 0.5.
   - Session 2 offers only the unfinished remainder, at 3 RIR or more. In the usual case, where session 1 gave the full dose, that remainder is zero and the muscle sits out.
   - Never a second full dose.
3. **Novices:** muscles trained today always sit out.

These defaults rest on:
- the two-a-day trials, which split a fixed daily dose;
- the recovery data: near-failure work takes 24 to 48 h, while 3 RIR left nothing measurable at 24 h;
- Morin 2023 and Takegaki 2019, where repeating hard work too soon backfired;
- the per-session plateau in the preprint.

The "one normal session" cap and the 3-RIR figure are defaults informed by this evidence. No study fixed those values.

### Questions for the owner (the evidence cannot decide these)
1. **Catch-up length.** Should a catch-up give 2 or 3 sets per muscle, and include how many muscles? Any number of at least 2 sets is supported, with diminishing returns beyond that. This is a session-length decision.
2. **"Behind" early in the week.** On a Monday every muscle is below half its target, so a catch-up becomes a full-body session. Because only weekly totals matter, ranking by projected shortfall (sets done plus sets still planned, against the target) fits the evidence better. This is product logic.
3. **The 48-h rule after a catch-up.** As the rule stands, a catch-up today benches that muscle from tomorrow's planned session, so it moves sets rather than adding them. Sets stopped at 3 RIR or more showed no carryover at 24 h (Refalo 2023, González-Badillo 2016, Raastad & Hallén 2000). That would support a 24-h sit-out for light catch-up sets, but it changes an existing rule.
4. **Item 32: the remainder rule or pure exclusion.** The evidence cannot separate their outcomes. The remainder rule honours "go easy" and covers a first session that was cut short; pure exclusion is simpler.
5. **Who counts as a novice.** Damage falls by week 3 and is minimal by week 10 (Damas 2016). Returning lifters after a long break may behave the same way. No validated cut-off exists.

### Uncertainties
- **Two-a-day evidence.** There are five small studies (n=8 to 23) lasting 1 to 8 weeks, all in athletes. Two used sequential periods rather than randomisation, and none added a second full dose.
- **Recovery studies.** Most are small (n=6 to 30), in young trained men, on squat or bench press. They measure performance, not growth. Recovery varies by exercise (Belcher), by sex (Refalo found larger fatigue in males) and by training status.
- **The per-session plateau** comes from a preprint, and a "session" is not the same as a day.
- **Size of the effect.** The effect of a single catch-up is too small to measure; the case for it rests on cumulative weekly volume. Most of the underlying evidence is from novices, which ACSM 2026 notes.
- **App conventions.** The 48-h and half-target thresholds are the app's own conventions. The evidence ties recovery to effort and exercise, not to a fixed clock.
