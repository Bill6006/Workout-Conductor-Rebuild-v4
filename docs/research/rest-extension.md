# The coach's longer rest (the owner's item 38)

Seen on the live app on 2026-09-25 and built in Maintenance 25 (round G). The owner: "After I accept
this recommendation successfully, it currently appears to remain actionable and lets me keep adding
another 30 seconds. Treat the recommendation as a one-time action for that condition."

## What it did

When the last two sets of the lift in front lost two reps or more and the latest had nothing in
reserve, the coach offered "Rest 30 s longer before the next X set". The tap added 30 s to the
running rest and nothing else: the offer was never marked as taken, and the same two sets raised it
again, so each tap added another 30 s (150, 180, 210, 240 s in the test that found it). With no rest
running, a tap did nothing at all.

## What the research says

Every paper was checked on PubMed (E-utilities) on 2026-09-25.

- With loads between 50% and 90% of the one-rep max, rests of 3 to 5 minutes kept more reps over
  several sets than short ones (de Salles, Simão, Miranda, Novaes, Lemos and Willardson, 2009,
  Sports Medicine, PMID 19691365; Willardson, 2006, Journal of Strength and Conditioning Research,
  PMID 17194236).
- Rests over two minutes gave trained lifters the most strength; 60 to 120 s was enough for the
  untrained (Grgic, Schoenfeld, Skrepnik, Davies and Mikulic, 2018, Sports Medicine, systematic
  review, PMID 28933024).
- Rests over 60 s gave a little more muscle growth, and over 90 s no clear difference, most likely
  because the loss of volume load levels off there (Singer, Wolf, Generoso, Arias, Delcastillo,
  Echevarria, Martinez, Androulakis Korakakis, Refalo, Swinton and Schoenfeld, 2024, Frontiers in
  Sports and Active Living, Bayesian meta-analysis, PMID 39205815).

A longer rest after reps fall away helps keep the next set in range, the reason the coach gives. A
set that falls away again after that rest is new evidence about today.

## The design that follows

1. The offer is about one rest: the rest after the set that fell short, while it runs.
2. Taking it adds the 30 s and marks the offer as taken for that rest, in one change; the card goes,
   and a second tap before it went adds nothing.
3. A later set that falls short again starts a new rest and can bring the offer back for that rest.
   Each rest gets at most one: the extra 30 s never stacks on one rest.
4. Once the rest is over the offer goes, and a tap that lands after it changes nothing and says so:
   "That rest is over: the next set can start."
